🧩 Rules for the group work

Ten rules that help 11th grade students work together respectfully. Each rule has a short note on what it looks like in a lesson.

Before we start

1
Divide the work fairly.

Give every member a role and a part of the task before you begin.

2
Use time wisely.

Plan the steps first, then decide how many minutes each step gets.

While we work

3
Listen without interrupting.

Let the speaker finish. Then ask a question or add your idea.

4
Use polite language.

Disagree with the idea, not with the person: “I see it differently, because…”

5
Invite quiet members to speak.

Ask “What do you think?” so that every voice in the group is heard.

6
Stay on the task.

Talk about the task, not about other plans. Phones are only for research.

7
Do your own part.

Finish your piece of work and do not wait for others to do it for you.

8
Support each other.

If someone is stuck, explain the step. Do not do it for them.

At the end

9
Keep to the deadline.

The timekeeper warns the group five minutes before the end.

10
Check the final work together.

Read the result as a group. Every member must be able to explain it.

Group roles

Roles change every lesson, so every student tries each one.

  • Leader keeps the group on the task and makes sure everyone takes part.
  • Timekeeper watches the time and tells the group how many minutes are left.
  • Speaker presents the group’s result to the class.
  • Secretary writes down the group’s ideas and the final answer.

Useful phrases

  • Could you say more about that?
  • I see your point, but…
  • What do you think, Dana?
  • Let’s get back to the task.
  • We have five minutes left.
  • Can everyone explain this part?

🌟 Reading tasks for gifted and talented

Slide 1 · Source

English Plus 6, page 34

Grade: 6
Book: English Plus (Grade 6) Student’s Book, Kazakhstan Edition. Ben Wetz, Diana Pye. Oxford University Press, 2015
Unit: 3 “Our countryside”, Reading “The red list”
Page: 34 · Text: “Danger! Humans!”

Open the book on okulyk.kz (page 34 in the online reader)

Slide 2 · Original task

What the book asks

  1. Read the title of the article and the question. What do you think the answer is? Why? Read and listen to the text and check your answer.
  2. Read the text again. Choose the correct answers. (five questions with a, b or c, for example “Sharks attack ___ people every year. a 100 b 7 or 8 c 3 or 4”)
  3. Build your vocabulary. Look at the words in blue in the text. Which two words are verbs? Use the words to complete the sentences.
  4. About you. Answer the questions. Then compare your answers with a partner.

These tasks check that students found the information and know the words. A gifted reader finishes them in a few minutes.

Slide 3 · My tasks

For gifted and talented learners, grade 6

  1. Analyse the numbers. The writer puts 100 million sharks next to “three or four” people, and says that more people die in accidents with chairs. Why does the writer put these numbers together? What do they want the reader to feel?
  2. Fact or opinion? Find two facts and two opinions in the text. Which words show that a sentence is an opinion, for example “It’s important…”?
  3. Evaluate. The writer says that people are usually more dangerous than animals. Do you agree? Give two reasons from the text and one reason of your own.
  4. Research and create. Choose one animal from Kazakhstan’s red list in the text: a horse, leopard, wild dog, lynx, otter, bat or bear. Find out where it lives and why it is in danger. Make a fact file of 80–100 words with one action your class can take. Use at least two of the blue words from the text.
Slide 4 · Why it is different

Book vs my tasks

Book: find information, choose a, b or c, fill the gaps, talk about yourself. This is remembering and understanding.

Mine: analyse how the writer persuades, separate facts from opinions, judge the writer’s main idea, research and create a fact file. These are the higher levels of Bloom’s taxonomy: analyse, evaluate, create.

Why it suits gifted 6th graders: they read the same text as the class, so they stay in the lesson topic, but they work deeper and more independently. The tasks are open, need research and end with their own written product.

✍️ Essay on underachievers

"The reasons of underachievement of pupils"

Every class has a child who could do well but somehow does not. I think the reasons are rarely about intelligence. The first one is the home. When parents are too busy, or never talk about school, a pupil quickly decides that studying is not important. Money problems and a noisy flat without a place to do homework can have the same effect.

The second reason is motivation. Pupils who failed a few times start to believe "I am just bad at this", and then they stop trying, because not trying hurts less than failing. Teaching matters too. A lesson that is boring, or where the teacher only works with the best students, loses the rest in a few weeks.

Finally, there is stress. Bullying, anxiety and tiredness take attention away from the lesson, and nobody notices because the child stays quiet.

In my view underachievement is nearly always a mix of causes. Parents, teachers and the school have to notice it early and talk to the pupil, not just give a bad mark.

📝 Memo on Lesson Study

TO: Teachers of the English department
FROM: Khaldar Aigerim
RE: Introducing Lesson Study

1. What it is. Lesson Study is a way for teachers to improve together. A small group plans one "research lesson", one teacher teaches it, the others watch how students learn, and then everyone discusses what they saw.

2. Origin. It comes from Japan, where it is called jugyō kenkyū, and became known worldwide through Stigler and Hiebert's book The Teaching Gap (1999).

3. The cycle. Plan together; teach and observe; discuss the evidence; improve the lesson and teach it again to another class.

4. Why it helps. The focus is on students' learning, not on judging the teacher. It builds trust and gives real evidence instead of opinions.

5. Proposal. One cycle per term, a team of three teachers, results shared at the methodical meeting.

💬 Writing a short review – result of the questionnaire

Results of the questionnaire from task 9: 14 responses. Each chart is followed by my analysis, and the short review is at the end.

Question 1

To what extent do you consider inclusive education an indispensable component of an equitable and socially just educational system?

Analysis: Ten of the 14 respondents (71.4%) chose “Absolutely essential”, three (21.4%) said it is moderately important and one (7.1%) called it highly important but not indispensable. Nobody chose “Of limited importance” or “I have no definite opinion”, so every respondent had a view and almost all of them see inclusion as a basic part of fair education.

Question 2

Do you believe that integrating learners with diverse educational needs into mainstream classrooms genuinely promotes social inclusion, or does it merely create the appearance of equality?

Analysis: Eight people (57.1%) think integration genuinely promotes social inclusion. The other six are split evenly: three (21.4%) say it only creates the appearance of equality without practical support, and three (21.4%) say the result depends on implementation. So more than 40% are not sure that putting children in one classroom is enough by itself.

Question 3

To what extent should educational institutions be held accountable for eliminating structural barriers that prevent students with disabilities from accessing quality education?

Analysis: Eight respondents (57.1%) believe schools are entirely accountable for removing these barriers. Three (21.4%) share the responsibility between schools and the government, and three (21.4%) between schools, families and society. Nobody chose “Only partially accountable” or “Not accountable”: all 14 expect schools to act.

Question 4

Which factor do you consider the most significant obstacle to the successful implementation of inclusive education?

Analysis: Half of the respondents (7, 50%) named insufficient professional training for teachers. Four (28.6%) pointed to social stigma, two (14.3%) to a lack of money and resources, and one (7.1%) to weak policies. Nobody chose inaccessible infrastructure. In this group the main obstacle is people’s preparation and attitudes, not buildings.

Question 5

Do you agree that providing identical educational opportunities to all learners is insufficient unless their individual needs and circumstances are taken into consideration?

Analysis: Six people strongly agreed (42.9%) and six agreed (42.9%), which makes 12 of 14 (85.7%). Two (14.3%) stayed neutral and nobody disagreed. The respondents clearly separate equal opportunities from equal treatment: giving everyone the same is not enough.

Question 6

To what extent can differentiated instruction and individualised learning strategies reconcile academic standards with the diverse abilities of students?

Analysis: This is the most cautious result. The largest group, six people (42.9%), chose “To a moderate extent”. Five (35.7%) answered “To a very great extent” and three (21.4%) “To a considerable extent”. Nobody chose the two negative options, so respondents trust differentiation but do not think it solves the problem completely.

Question 7

Who should bear the primary responsibility for ensuring the effective implementation of inclusive education?

Analysis: Six respondents (42.9%) gave the main responsibility to the government and educational authorities, and five (35.7%) think all stakeholders should share it equally. Two (14.3%) chose school administrations and only one (7.1%) chose teachers. Nobody chose parents.

Question 8

Do you think that the presence of students with special educational needs in mainstream classrooms may require a fundamental reconsideration of conventional teaching methodologies and assessment practices?

Analysis: Nine people (64.3%) strongly agreed and two (14.3%) agreed, so 11 of 14 (78.6%) think teaching and assessment have to change. Three (21.4%) were neutral and nobody disagreed. This is the strongest agreement in the whole questionnaire.

Question 9

In your opinion, what measures would most effectively foster a genuinely inclusive educational environment rather than merely fulfil formal institutional requirements?

Analysis: Eight respondents (57.1%) chose continuous professional development for teachers, five (35.7%) accessible infrastructure and assistive technologies, and one (7.1%) collaboration between schools, families and specialists. This matches question 4, where training was the main obstacle. It is interesting that infrastructure was not seen as an obstacle there, but five people still chose it as a useful measure here.

Question 10

How would you assess the long-term societal implications of implementing inclusive education on a large scale?

Analysis: Seven people (50%) expect inclusive education to strengthen social cohesion and mutual understanding, and six (42.9%) expect more equal opportunities and less inequality. Only one (7.1%) said the consequences are difficult to predict. So 13 of 14 respondents expect a positive effect on society.

Overview

Fourteen people answered my questionnaire, and most of them support inclusive education. Ten called it absolutely essential, and nobody disagreed that teaching and assessment have to change when children with special educational needs join a mainstream class.

The answers are not naive, though. Three respondents think inclusion can create only the appearance of equality, and the largest group believes differentiated instruction can balance standards and abilities only to a moderate extent.

The clearest result is about teachers. Half of the respondents named weak professional training as the main obstacle, and eight chose continuous professional development as the best measure. Yet only one person thinks teachers should carry the main responsibility; most expect it from the government or from everyone together.

For me as a future teacher, this means that a positive attitude is not enough. I need practical skills in differentiation and fair assessment, and schools need real support from the authorities, so that inclusion works in everyday lessons and not only in documents.
